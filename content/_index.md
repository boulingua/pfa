## What this is

Persian is spoken by upwards of a hundred million people and taught almost
nowhere in the German school system, despite a large heritage population from
Iran and Afghanistan sitting in its classrooms. What free material exists is
mostly a phrasebook, a font demo, or a grammar table with no route through it.
This course is the other thing: a structured, CEFR-traceable syllabus with
units, exams, slide decks, worksheets and native-voice audio, in the native
script from the first page, published as a static site under an open licence.

Every unit follows the same five moves — **Objectives → Input → Practise →
Produce → Reflect** — and carries an explicit list of the can-do descriptors it
realises. Exams are first-class pages, not appendices: each block of three to
four units closes with one, and each level closes with a full model exam.

## Who this is for

Teachers who need a ready-to-teach skeleton for a Persian course or a
heritage-language group, and independent learners who want a route rather than
a reference. No prior knowledge of the Perso-Arabic script is assumed; the
script stage exists precisely so that the A1 units can be taught in real
Persian orthography instead of in transliteration.

## Three decisions that shape the course

**The script comes first, and it is never abandoned.** Persian is normally
written without short-vowel marks. Teaching it in transliteration and switching
later produces learners who cannot read a shop sign. So the course marks short
vowels fully in the script stage and early A1, then drops them — the same fade
that real reading requires. A light Latin transliteration accompanies new
vocabulary through A1 only.

**The *ezāfe* is taught as grammar, not as a footnote.** The unstressed linker
between a noun and what modifies it — {{< fa >}}کتابِ سارا{{< /fa >}}, *ketāb-e
Sārā*, "Sara's book" — is almost always unwritten and always spoken. It has no
German or English analogue, it silently underlies most everyday syntax, and it
is therefore an A1 unit of its own rather than a remark in a vocabulary list.

**Formal and colloquial are both taught, and always labelled.** The distance
between written {{< fa >}}می‌روم{{< /fa >}} (*mi-ravam*) and spoken
{{< fa >}}می‌رم{{< /fa >}} (*mi-ram*) is wider than anything in a European
classroom language. The written-formal form is the reference spine of the
course; the colloquial form is introduced deliberately, marked as such, from A2
onwards. The full convention is set out in the
[course conventions](/appendices/style-guide/).

## Scope, stated honestly

The declared conformance target is **`core`** — CEFR **A1 to B1**, plus the
pre-A1 script stage. B2, C1 and C2 are **declared gaps**, not silently missing
levels: openly-licensed, CEFR-traceable Persian material thins out sharply
beyond the intermediate range, and closing that gap properly is a multi-year
project. Dari (Afghanistan) and Tajik (written in Cyrillic) are related but
separately standardised varieties; they are named and situated in the cultural
notes, and they are not taught here.

## Where the course stands

The site, the design system, the right-to-left layer and the legal and
counting-mark scaffolding are in place. Authoring runs stage by stage: the
script stage first, then A1 — the point at which this course stops being marked
"coming soon" — then A2 and B1. Each level lands complete: units, exams,
committed slide decks and worksheets, audio, and a machine-readable coverage
manifest that must pass the curriculum framework's identifier audit before the
level is called done.
