## 1. Objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

- explain why a Persian letter has up to four shapes, and identify the same
  letter across all of them;
- read and write the nine letters {{< fa >}}ا ب ت د ر س م ن و{{< /fa >}} in
  isolation and in combination;
- say which of those letters refuse to join to the letter after them, and show
  what that does to the shape of a word;
- decode fourteen everyday Persian words letter by letter and read them aloud;
- write the name {{< fa >}}سارا{{< /fa >}} (*Sārā*) by hand.

## 2. Input

### 2.1 One letter, several shapes

The single fact that makes Perso-Arabic script look harder than it is: **a
letter's shape depends on its neighbours, its identity does not.** Persian is
written in a joined hand — what Latin script does only in cursive, Persian does
always, in print as well. A letter therefore appears in up to four shapes:

- **isolated** — nothing joins to it on either side;
- **initial** — it joins forward, to the letter that follows;
- **medial** — it is joined on both sides;
- **final** — something joins into it from behind, but it joins nothing forward.

Compare the letter *nun* in three positions: {{< fa >}}ن{{< /fa >}} on its own,
{{< fa >}}نان{{< /fa >}} (*nān*, "bread") where it opens the word as
{{< fa >}}نـ{{< /fa >}} and closes it as {{< fa >}}ـن{{< /fa >}}. Two shapes,
one letter, one sound. The dot above it never moves and never changes: **dots
carry identity, the skeleton carries position.**

### 2.2 The nine letters of this unit

| Letter | Name | Sound | Joins forward? |
|---|---|---|---|
| {{< fa >}}ا{{< /fa >}} | *alef* | long *ā*, or a carrier for a short vowel | no |
| {{< fa >}}ب{{< /fa >}} | *be* | *b* | yes |
| {{< fa >}}ت{{< /fa >}} | *te* | *t* | yes |
| {{< fa >}}د{{< /fa >}} | *dāl* | *d* | no |
| {{< fa >}}ر{{< /fa >}} | *re* | tapped *r* | no |
| {{< fa >}}س{{< /fa >}} | *sin* | *s* | yes |
| {{< fa >}}م{{< /fa >}} | *mim* | *m* | yes |
| {{< fa >}}ن{{< /fa >}} | *nun* | *n* | yes |
| {{< fa >}}و{{< /fa >}} | *vāv* | *v*; also long *u* and the diphthong *ow* | no |

The alphabet has thirty-two letters in all. These nine come first because they
are frequent, visually distinct, and enough to build real words on the first
day. The remaining letters arrive in Units 3 and 4, sorted by shape family
rather than by alphabetical order — the dictionary order is learnt later, when
there is something to look up.

### 2.3 The four positional forms

| Letter | Isolated | Initial | Medial | Final |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *be* | {{< fa >}}ب{{< /fa >}} | {{< fa >}}بـ{{< /fa >}} | {{< fa >}}ـبـ{{< /fa >}} | {{< fa >}}ـب{{< /fa >}} |
| *te* | {{< fa >}}ت{{< /fa >}} | {{< fa >}}تـ{{< /fa >}} | {{< fa >}}ـتـ{{< /fa >}} | {{< fa >}}ـت{{< /fa >}} |
| *sin* | {{< fa >}}س{{< /fa >}} | {{< fa >}}سـ{{< /fa >}} | {{< fa >}}ـسـ{{< /fa >}} | {{< fa >}}ـس{{< /fa >}} |
| *mim* | {{< fa >}}م{{< /fa >}} | {{< fa >}}مـ{{< /fa >}} | {{< fa >}}ـمـ{{< /fa >}} | {{< fa >}}ـم{{< /fa >}} |
| *nun* | {{< fa >}}ن{{< /fa >}} | {{< fa >}}نـ{{< /fa >}} | {{< fa >}}ـنـ{{< /fa >}} | {{< fa >}}ـن{{< /fa >}} |

The small stroke {{< fa >}}ـ{{< /fa >}} in the table is the connecting line, not
part of the letter. It is printed here so that the joining edge is visible; it
is never written on its own.

### 2.4 The letters that never join forward

Four of this unit's nine letters — {{< fa >}}ا{{< /fa >}},
{{< fa >}}د{{< /fa >}}, {{< fa >}}ر{{< /fa >}} and {{< fa >}}و{{< /fa >}} —
accept a join from the letter before them but pass nothing on to the letter
after them. They therefore have two shapes, not four:

| Letter | Isolated | Final (joined from behind) |
|---|---|---|
| *alef* | {{< fa >}}ا{{< /fa >}} | {{< fa >}}ـا{{< /fa >}} |
| *dāl* | {{< fa >}}د{{< /fa >}} | {{< fa >}}ـد{{< /fa >}} |
| *re* | {{< fa >}}ر{{< /fa >}} | {{< fa >}}ـر{{< /fa >}} |
| *vāv* | {{< fa >}}و{{< /fa >}} | {{< fa >}}ـو{{< /fa >}} |

There are seven such letters in the whole alphabet —
{{< fa >}}ا د ذ ر ز ژ و{{< /fa >}} — and Unit 2 is about the consequence: a
word is not one continuous ribbon of ink but a sequence of **blocks**, broken
wherever a non-joiner falls. In {{< fa >}}مادَر{{< /fa >}} (*mādar*, "mother")
the *mim* joins the *alef*, and then the line stops twice: the *alef* passes
nothing to the *dāl*, and the *dāl* passes nothing to the *re*. Three blocks,
four letters, one word.

### 2.5 First words

Every word below is written with its short vowels marked, as the whole script
stage is. Where no mark appears above or below a consonant, the vowel you hear
is a long one, already carried by {{< fa >}}ا{{< /fa >}} or
{{< fa >}}و{{< /fa >}}.

| {{< fa >}}نام{{< /fa >}} | *nām* | name | {{< fa >}}ن + ا + م{{< /fa >}} |
| {{< fa >}}باد{{< /fa >}} | *bād* | wind | {{< fa >}}ب + ا + د{{< /fa >}} |
| {{< fa >}}باران{{< /fa >}} | *bārān* | rain | {{< fa >}}ب + ا + ر + ا + ن{{< /fa >}} |
| {{< fa >}}دوست{{< /fa >}} | *dust* | friend | {{< fa >}}د + و + س + ت{{< /fa >}} |
| {{< fa >}}مادَر{{< /fa >}} | *mādar* | mother | {{< fa >}}م + ا + د + ر{{< /fa >}} |

### 2.6 Which way the line runs

Persian runs **right to left**. The first letter of a word sits at its right
edge, and a page turns the other way. Numbers are the exception and run left to
right inside the right-to-left line, which is why a Persian sentence containing
a year looks like it changes its mind halfway through. It does not: two
directions, one line, and Unit 7 deals with the arithmetic of it.

## 3. Practise

### A — Identify the letter

Each row shows the same letter in two positions. Name it.

1. {{< fa >}}بـ{{< /fa >}} · {{< fa >}}ـب{{< /fa >}}
2. {{< fa >}}سـ{{< /fa >}} · {{< fa >}}ـسـ{{< /fa >}}
3. {{< fa >}}نـ{{< /fa >}} · {{< fa >}}ـن{{< /fa >}}
4. {{< fa >}}مـ{{< /fa >}} · {{< fa >}}ـم{{< /fa >}}
5. {{< fa >}}ـا{{< /fa >}} · {{< fa >}}ا{{< /fa >}}

### B — Split the word

Write out the letters of each word, right to left, in isolated form.

1. {{< fa >}}نان{{< /fa >}} 2. {{< fa >}}دوست{{< /fa >}} 3. {{< fa >}}مادَر{{< /fa >}}
4. {{< fa >}}باران{{< /fa >}} 5. {{< fa >}}تُند{{< /fa >}}

### C — Join the letters

Join each string into a single written word, and say how many **blocks** it has.

1. {{< fa >}}ن + ا + م{{< /fa >}}
2. {{< fa >}}ب + ا + د{{< /fa >}}
3. {{< fa >}}س + ا + ر + ا{{< /fa >}}
4. {{< fa >}}ب + ر + ا + د + ر{{< /fa >}}
5. {{< fa >}}م + ر + د{{< /fa >}}

### D — Read aloud

Read the column down, then cover the transliteration and read it again.

نان · دَر · مَن · سَر · باد · دوست · مادَر

**B.** 1. {{< fa >}}ن + ا + ن{{< /fa >}} — 2. {{< fa >}}د + و + س + ت{{< /fa >}} —
3. {{< fa >}}م + ا + د + ر{{< /fa >}} — 4. {{< fa >}}ب + ا + ر + ا + ن{{< /fa >}} —
5. {{< fa >}}ت + ن + د{{< /fa >}}.

**C.** 1. {{< fa >}}نام{{< /fa >}}, two blocks ({{< fa >}}نا{{< /fa >}} + {{< fa >}}م{{< /fa >}}) —
2. {{< fa >}}باد{{< /fa >}}, two blocks —
3. {{< fa >}}سارا{{< /fa >}}, three blocks — {{< fa >}}سا{{< /fa >}} · {{< fa >}}ر{{< /fa >}} · {{< fa >}}ا{{< /fa >}}. The *alef* passes nothing to the *re*, and the *re* passes nothing to the second *alef* —
4. {{< fa >}}برادر{{< /fa >}}, four blocks — {{< fa >}}بر{{< /fa >}} · {{< fa >}}ا{{< /fa >}} · {{< fa >}}د{{< /fa >}} · {{< fa >}}ر{{< /fa >}} —
5. {{< fa >}}مرد{{< /fa >}}, two blocks.

**D.** Open — read for accuracy of the long *ā* in {{< fa >}}نان{{< /fa >}} and
{{< fa >}}باد{{< /fa >}}, not for speed.

## 4. Produce

**The name line.** Copy the name {{< fa >}}سارا{{< /fa >}} five times by hand,
right to left, starting each attempt from the *sin*. Then write it once without
looking. Sārā is a student in Tehran and the first member of the course's
recurring cast; she and Kāve, who runs a family bookshop near the bazaar, come
back throughout Levels A1 to B1, so her name is worth having in the hand early.

**The five-word card.** Choose five words from §2.5, write each one on a card in
Persian on one side and in English on the other, and read the Persian side
aloud to a partner. The partner names the letters, not the meaning.

**One sentence about the shape.** In English, in two or three lines: pick one
word from the list and describe how it is built — which letters join, where the
line breaks, and why. Describing the mechanics in your own words is what makes
the next twenty letters fast instead of slow.

- [ ] I can say which four of them never join forward, and show the effect on a word.
- [ ] I can read the fourteen words of §2.5 aloud without the transliteration.

**One line in your notebook:** the word from this unit whose shape you found
hardest to see, and what finally made it readable.

## Common pitfalls

- Treating a shape as a new letter. {{< fa >}}ـسـ{{< /fa >}} is not a different
  letter from {{< fa >}}س{{< /fa >}}; count the dots and the skeleton, not the
  silhouette.
- Trying to join forward from {{< fa >}}ا د ر و{{< /fa >}}. The break after a
  non-joiner is not a space and not the end of the word — Unit 2 shows the
  difference, and Unit 8 adds the invisible character that marks it.
- Reading {{< fa >}}و{{< /fa >}} as *v* everywhere. In
  {{< fa >}}دوست{{< /fa >}} it is the long vowel *u*; the same letter carries a
  consonant, a long vowel and a diphthong, and only the word tells you which.
- Writing the dots first. Write the skeleton of the whole word, then add dots
  and vowel marks — that is the order a Persian hand uses, and it is why dots
  drift when the order is reversed.
- Left-to-right habits. Starting a letter on its left edge produces shapes that
  look right in print and fall apart in cursive.

## Cultural note

The four letters {{< fa >}}پ چ ژ گ{{< /fa >}}, which Unit 4 introduces, are
Persian additions to an alphabet borrowed from Arabic for sounds Arabic does
not have. They are the visible seam between the writing system and the language
it was fitted to — and the reason a reader of Arabic can sound out a Persian
sign without understanding it, and a reader of Persian can do the same with an
Arabic one.
